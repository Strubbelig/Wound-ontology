# What Is a Wound? Building an Ontologic Foundation for Reconstructive Surgery

**Purpose:** Plastic surgeons treat wounds daily, yet the word "wound" is used inconsistently across clinical documentation, research studies, operative planning, registries, and reimbursement systems. A diabetic plantar ulcer, irradiated scalp defect, traumatic lower-extremity soft-tissue loss, pressure injury, and post-oncologic composite defect may all be called wounds, despite fundamentally different biology, reconstructive requirements, and outcome expectations. This imprecision limits communication, weakens comparative research, and constrains the development of artificial intelligence and decision-support tools in reconstructive surgery. We propose that formally defining a wound as an ontology can create a shared, computable language for plastic surgery.

**Methods:** We developed a conceptual wound ontology by synthesizing reconstructive surgical reasoning with existing clinical descriptors used in wound care, trauma, oncology, infection, vascular disease, and microsurgical reconstruction. Core domains included anatomic location, tissue components lost, depth and dimensionality, etiology, vascularity, contamination or infection, chronicity, host factors, exposed critical structures, reconstructive intent, and stage within the wound-healing or reconstruction pathway. Representative clinical scenarios were mapped to this framework to test whether the ontology could distinguish wounds that appear similar descriptively but differ surgically.

**Results:** The proposed framework separates "wound" into interrelated features rather than a single label. For example, a lower-extremity wound can be represented not only by size and location, but by mechanism, zone of injury, perfusion status, exposed tendon or bone, bacterial burden, prior radiation, systemic healing capacity, and reconstructive endpoint. This structure supports more precise comparison between cases, clearer operative indications, improved registry design, and machine-readable documentation for future predictive modeling.

**Conclusions:** For reconstructive surgeons, defining a wound ontologically is not an abstract informatics exercise; it is a way to encode the clinical logic already used at the bedside and in the operating room. A shared wound ontology could improve communication, research reproducibility, outcomes measurement, and AI readiness across plastic surgery. As reconstructive care becomes increasingly data-driven, the specialty has an opportunity to lead by defining the basic unit of its work: the wound itself.
